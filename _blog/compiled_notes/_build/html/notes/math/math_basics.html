Math basics#

Assorted math basics - functions, discrete math, proofs, identities, and complex numbers.

About this chapter

A grab bag of facts that the other math chapters use without stating. It starts with a few bounds, such as Stirling’s formula for \(n!\), and a short list of named functions (gamma, sigmoid, softmax). Then come one-line definitions from stochastic processes, abstract algebra and discrete math, and a list of proof techniques.

The last sections are reference material: log and trig identities, partial fractions, and complex numbers. Polar form \(z = |z| e^{i\theta}\) is the one idea worth slowing down for, since it is what makes multiplying complex numbers easy and it comes back in the Fourier transform.

Misc#

Bounds that come up when counting and when analyzing algorithms. Stirling’s formula says \(n!\) grows like \((n/e)^n\), so \(\log(n!)\) is \(O(n \log n)\).

  • \(\left( \frac{n}{k} \right) < \left( \frac{ne}{k} \right)^k\)

  • Stirling’s formula: \( n! ~= (\frac{n}{e})^n \)

    • corollary: log(n!) = 0(n log n)

    • gives us a bound on sorting

    • \(\left( \frac{n}{e} \right)^n < n!\)

  • \((1-x)^N \leq e^{-Nx}\)

  • Poisson pmf approximates binomial when N large, p small

Functions#

Named functions that show up in later chapters. The gamma function extends the factorial, \(\Gamma(n) = (n-1)!\), and the sigmoid and softmax turn real numbers into probabilities.

  • Gamma: \(\Gamma(n)=(n-1)!=\int_0^\infty x^{n-1}e^{-x}dx\)

  • Zeta: \(\zeta(x) = \sum_1^\infty \frac{1}{x^2} \)

  • Sigmoid (logistic): \(f(x) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-x}} = \frac{e^x}{e^x+1}\)

  • Softmax: \(f(x) = \frac{e^{x_i}}{\sum_i e^{x_i}}\)

  • spline: piecewise polynomial

Stochastic processes#

A stochastic process is a random variable evolving in time. The Markov property says the next value depends on the past only through the current value.

  • Stochastic - random process evolving with time

  • Markov: \(P(X_t=x\vert X_{t-1})=P(X_t=x\vert X_{t-1}...X_1)\)

  • Martingale: \(E[X_t]=X_{t-1}\)

Abstract algebra#

  • Group: set of elements endowed with operation satisfying 4 properties:

  1. closed 2. identity 3. associative 4. inverses

  • Equivalence Relation;

  1. reflexive 2. transitive 3. symmetric

Discrete math#

Definitions for sets and propositional logic, then the standard ways to prove an implication \(p \to q\): directly, by contradiction, by the contrapositive, or by induction.

  • Goldbach’s strong conjecture: Every even integer greater than 2 can be expressed as the sum of two primes (He considered one a prime).

  • Goldbach’s weak conjecture: All odd numbers greater than 7 are the sum of three primes.

  • Set - An unordered collection of items without replication

  • Proper subset - subset with cardinality less than the set

    • A U A = A Idempotent law

  • Disjoint: A and B = empty set

  • Partition: mutually disjoint, union fills space

  • powerset \(\mathcal{P}\)(A) = set of all subsets

  • Converse: \(q\to p\) (same as inverse: \(-p \to -q\))

  • \(p_1 \to p_2 \iff - p_1 \lor p_2 \)

  • The greatest common divisor of two integers a and b is the largest integer d such that d \(\vert\) a and d \(\vert\) b

  • Proof Techniques

    • Proof by Induction

    • Direct Proof

    • Proof by Contradiction - assume p \(\land\) -q, show contradiction

    • Proof by Contrapositive - show -q \(\to\) -p

Proofs#

Notes on what proofs can and can’t do: induction checks a formula you already have but doesn’t explain it, and a non-existence proof has to rule out every case. The subsection lists famous results and puzzles, with a worked pigeonhole argument.

  • induction

    • must already know formula

    • doesn’t give intuition

    • there are uncomputable functions e.g. Halting Problem, 3x+1 problem

  • non-existence proofs

    • must cover all possible scenarios, harder than existence

  • Pigeonhole Principle

    • Claim: Consider a 10 foot by 10 foot square room. Let every point on the floor of the room be colored either red or blue. Then there exists somewhere on the floor two points of the same color that are exactly one foot apart.

    • Proof: Consider an equilateral triangle on the floor of the room with side lengths equal to one foot. As every point is either red or blue, two vertices of the triangle must have the same color, satisfying the claim.

Identities#

Log rules, change of base, partial fractions and the angle-sum formulas for sine and cosine. Change of base is why logs in different bases differ only by a constant factor.

  • \(e^{-2lnx}= \frac{1}{e^{2lnx}} = \frac{1}{e^{lnx}e^{lnx}} = \frac{1}{x^2}\)

  • \(\ln(xy) = \ln(x)+\ln(y)\)

  • \(\ln x * \ln y = \ln(x^{\ln y})\)

    • difference between log 10n and log 2n is always a constant (about 3.322)

  • \(\log_b (x) = \log_d (x) / \log_d (b)\)

  • partial fractions: \(\frac{3x+11}{(x-3)(x+2)} = \frac{A}{x-3} + \frac{B}{x+2}\)

  • \((ax+b)^k = \frac{A_1}{ax+b}+\frac{A_2}{(ax+b)^2}+...\)

  • \((ax^2+bx+c)^k = \frac{A_1x+B_1}{ax^2+bx+c}+...\)

  • \(\cos(a\pm b) = \cos(a)\cos(b)\mp \sin(a)\sin(b)\)

  • \(\sin(a \pm b) = \sin(a)\cos(b) \pm \sin(b)\cos(a)\)

Imaginary numbers#

Euler’s formula turns a complex number into a magnitude times a phase. In that form, multiplying two numbers multiplies their magnitudes and adds their angles.

  • complex conjugate of z=x+iy is \(z^*\) = x - iy

  • Euler’s formula \(e^{i \theta} = \cos (\theta) + i \sin (\theta)\)

  • sometimes we write imaginary numbers in polar form: \(z = \cblue{\vert z\vert} \corange{e^{i \theta}}\)

    • magnitude times phase

    • makes multiplication / division simpler

  • absolute value / modules of imaginary numbers: \(\vert a + ib\vert = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}\)

Spaces#

  • hilbert space - requires an inner product (useful in analyzing kernels) - more general than an inner product space

    • reproducing kernel hilbert space with extra property

Key ideas

  • Stirling’s formula is \(n!\approx (n/e)^n\), which gives \(\log(n!) = O(n \log n)\), the bound behind sorting.

  • The Poisson pmf approximates the binomial when \(N\) is large and \(p\) is small.

  • An implication \(p \to q\) can be proved directly, by contradiction, by the contrapositive \(\neg q \to \neg p\), or by induction.

  • Logs in different bases differ by a constant factor, \(\log_b x = \log_d x / \log_d b\).

  • In polar form \(z = |z| e^{i\theta}\), multiplying complex numbers multiplies magnitudes and adds angles.
